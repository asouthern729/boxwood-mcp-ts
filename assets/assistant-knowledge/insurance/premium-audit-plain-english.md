# Plain-English Reference

The whole point of this document is that the insured cannot read the carrier's audit statement.
Everything here exists to close that gap. Read this file before writing any narrative text.

## Contents
1. Class description expansion
2. Premium basis normalization
3. Standing boilerplate — "About This Audit"
4. Standing boilerplate — "What Happens Next"
5. Writing the "What Changed and Why" narrative

---

## 1. Class description expansion

Carrier audit statements abbreviate class descriptions to fit their own forms. Expand them into
something a business owner reads without stopping. Keep the carrier's wording recognizable —
the insured may be comparing side by side with the carrier statement — but spell out the
abbreviation.

| Carrier shorthand | Write instead |
|---|---|
| `NOC` | `Not Otherwise Classified` |
| `Exec Supervisor` | `Executive Supervisor` |
| `Sub Contracted Work` / `Subcontracted Work` | `Subcontracted Work` |
| `1 or 2 Family Dwellings` | `One- or Two-Family Dwellings` |
| `Prop` (in `Residential Prop`) | `Property` |
| `Bldgs` | `Buildings` |
| `Clerical Office Employees NOC` | `Clerical Office Employees` |
| `Salespersons, Collectors or Messengers — Outside` | `Outside Sales` |
| `Contractors — Executive Supervisors` | `Executive Supervisor` |

Use an em dash, not a hyphen, between the trade and its qualifier:
`Carpentry- NOC` → `Carpentry — Not Otherwise Classified`

Never change the class code number itself, and never merge two class codes into one row even if
their descriptions look similar. The code is what the carrier rated on.

## 2. Premium basis normalization

| Source value | Display as |
|---|---|
| `Payroll`, `PR`, `Remuneration` | `Payroll` |
| `Total Cost`, `Sub Cost`, `Cost` | `Subcontracted Cost` |
| `Sales`, `Gross Sales`, `Receipts` | `Gross Sales` |
| `Area`, `Sq Ft` | `Square Footage` |
| `Units`, `Each` | `Per Unit` |
| `Admissions` | `Admissions` |

The basis determines which annual total a row rolls into (see SKILL.md, "Annual totals"), so
normalize before totaling, not after.

## 3. Standing boilerplate — "About This Audit"

Use this text essentially verbatim. It's been written to explain the mechanic without sounding
defensive about the bill. Adjust only the policy-term and carrier specifics.

> Your commercial policies were written at the start of the term using estimated exposures —
> projected payroll, projected subcontractor costs, or projected sales. Because no one knows a
> year's actual figures in advance, every policy of this type is issued on an estimate and then
> reconciled at the end of the term through a premium audit.
>
> At the end of the policy period, the carrier reviews your actual records and recalculates the
> premium using the real numbers. If your actual exposures came in higher than the estimate, the
> carrier bills the difference as additional premium. If they came in lower, the carrier issues a
> return premium.
>
> The pages that follow show, line by line, what was estimated, what the audit found, and how
> that changed your premium.

If the account's exposure basis has nothing to do with payroll or subcontractors (a sales-rated
retail account, for example), swap those examples for the ones that actually apply rather than
leaving language that doesn't match the schedules below it.

## 4. Standing boilerplate — "What Happens Next"

Include these items when they apply. Keep them as short bullets, not paragraphs. The dispute
bullet is the exception: it always appears, and its wording is fixed.

- **The invoice.** The additional premium is billed by the carrier, not the agency. Watch for it
  directly from [carrier]. Terms are typically due on receipt or within 30 days depending on the
  carrier and whether the policy was financed.
- **If the policy was financed.** An audit balance is usually billed separately and is not
  automatically added to the existing finance agreement.
- **Disputing the result.** Always include this bullet, and use the wording below **exactly,
  word for word** — no rephrasing, no carrier-specific edits, no added deadlines or phone numbers.
  Boxwood has approved this exact text:

  > **Disputing the result.** If the audited figures don't match your records, let us know
  > quickly. Carriers allow a limited window to request a re-audit, and it's far easier to correct
  > before the balance ages.
- **Reducing next year's surprise.** Two things drive most audit bills on contracting accounts:
  subcontractor costs coming in above estimate, and uninsured subcontractors being charged back
  to your policy as if they were your own payroll.
  - Collect a certificate of insurance from every subcontractor before they start work, and keep
    it on file through the end of the policy term.
  - Keep payroll records that separate overtime, and separate by class of work, so the carrier
    can apply the correct rate rather than defaulting to the highest one.
- **Updating the estimate.** If this year's actual figures reflect a new normal for the business,
  the renewal estimates should be raised to match so the same gap doesn't repeat.

Only include the subcontractor bullets when the account actually has a subcontracted-cost basis
in the schedules. Don't lecture a clerical account about certificates of insurance.

## 5. Writing the "What Changed and Why" narrative

Three to five sentences per line of business, maximum. Ground every sentence in a number that
appears in a table on the same page — this section explains the tables, it does not introduce
new figures.

Lead with the single largest dollar driver, not the largest percentage. A class that moved from
$12,000,000 to $20,940,103 matters more than one that doubled off a $50,000 base.

Say what the movement means in business terms, not just that it happened:

- Good: "Subcontracted costs came in $8.3 million above the estimate, driven almost entirely by
  work on one- and two-family dwellings. Because General Liability for this class is rated on the
  cost of the work subcontracted out, that increase is the primary reason for the additional
  premium."
- Bad: "Class code 91583 increased from $12,000,000 to $20,940,103, a change of 74.5%."

When a class audited to zero, say so plainly and say what it means — that the work wasn't
performed during the term, so no premium was charged for it.

Do not editorialize about whether the carrier's result is fair, and do not speculate about
whether a re-audit would succeed. State what the numbers show.
